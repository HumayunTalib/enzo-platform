Much of Pakistan’s cloth trade still runs on paper slips and trust. Two words come up again and again: kachchi parchi and pakki parchi. Understanding them explains how a fabric deal is checked and settled, and why a careful buyer inspects metres and faults before the final amount is agreed. This guide explains both receipts, how fabric moves from Faisalabad to markets such as Karachi’s Kapra Market, and what the system means for buyers today.

Kachchi parchi and pakki parchi

In Urdu, kachcha means raw or provisional and pakka means firm or final. A parchi is a slip of paper. In the cloth trade the two receipts mark two stages of the same deal.

Dawn’s 2021 report on Faisalabad’s Sutar Mandi described the practice through a trader’s own words: “We issue kachchi parchi when we send fabric for checking at the mending section.” Once mending is complete, “a pakki parchi is issued to the supplier”, showing the total amount and the metres supplied, and payment is made through banks (Dawn, 30 May 2021).

Kachchi parchiPakki parchi
When it is issuedWhen cloth is sent for checking and mendingAfter mending is complete
What it recordsA provisional, plain-paper note of the goodsMetres supplied and total amount
What followsInspection and repair of faultsPayment, through banks in the trader’s account

The same report noted that yarn, by contrast, was still sold on kachchi parchi alone, with buyers paying the full amount.

Why checking comes before settling

Woven cloth comes off the loom with faults: broken ends, missing picks, slubs, oil stains and similar defects. Our guide to greige fabric defects describes the common ones. Mending is the step where workers go over the cloth, repair what can be repaired and mark what cannot.

The order of the receipts reflects that. The final quantity and amount are fixed only after the cloth has been checked. Metres can change as faulty sections are cut out or marked, and the value of a piece depends on how many faults remain. In practice, inspection is built into the deal. A buyer settling on the pakki parchi is settling on checked cloth, not on loom output.

How cloth moves: Faisalabad to Kapra Market

Faisalabad is a major centre of Pakistan’s weaving trade, and its yarn and cloth market brings together spinners, weavers, traders, brokers and commission agents (Dawn, 2021). Our overview of the Faisalabad textile industry covers the wider picture.

From there, cloth travels to wholesale markets across the country. Dawn’s report on Karachi’s Kapra Market, also known as Kaghzi Bazaar, said Faisalabad held a 95 per cent share of the fabric arriving there, with some from Gujranwala and Lahore. It estimated arrivals of basic dyed suiting and embroidered fabric from Faisalabad at 500,000 to 700,000 metres a day. Unstitched grey fabric also arrived, but in small volumes, because “mostly finished and dyed fabrics are finding their way in the market” (Dawn, 21 February 2021).

So a suit length bought in Karachi has usually passed through several hands: weaver, mending and checking, processor, trader and wholesaler. Each handover is a point where metres and quality can be checked, or missed. Lahore buyers will recognise the same chain; see Lahore fabric sourcing.

Where informal receipts create risk

Informal receipts are quick and flexible, and they work well between traders who know each other. They also have weaknesses that the same reporting records. Dawn quoted a loom owners’ association leader saying that kachchi parchi lets speculative trading happen several times a year, harming clean traders, and noted official warnings about “fake and flying invoices” (Dawn, 2021).

For a buyer, the practical risks are simpler:

  • A provisional slip may not state width, GSM or construction, so there is little to check against later.
  • If metres are not measured at handover, a short delivery is hard to prove.
  • If faults are not recorded before payment, any dispute becomes one person’s word against another’s.

What this means for buyers

The parchi system already teaches the right habit: check before you settle. Whether you buy greige by the lot or finished cloth by the piece, apply that habit consistently.

  1. Agree the specification first. Construction, blend, width, GSM and shade, in writing. See questions before a bulk fabric order.
  2. Measure metres yourself. Check the metres on a sample of pieces against what the slip says.
  3. Inspect for faults. Use a consistent method and record what you find. Our fabric inspection checklist and inspecting before a bulk order guides set out the steps.
  4. Settle on checked figures. Agree the final metres and amount only after inspection, and keep a written record.
  5. Know who you are dealing with. A mill and a trader carry different responsibilities; see fabric supplier vs trader.

Frequently asked questions

What is kachchi parchi in the cloth trade?

A provisional plain-paper receipt issued when fabric is sent for checking at the mending section, before the final quantity and amount are fixed.

What is pakki parchi?

The final receipt issued after mending, recording the metres supplied and the total amount. Payment follows.

Why is cloth sent for mending before payment?

Woven cloth carries loom faults. Mending repairs or marks them, which can change the usable metres and the value of the piece.

Where does Karachi’s Kapra Market get its fabric?

Dawn reported that about 95 per cent came from Faisalabad, with some from Gujranwala and Lahore, and that most of it arrived finished and dyed.

What should I check before settling a fabric deal?

Metres, width, faults and shade against the agreed specification, recorded in writing before the final amount is agreed.

Conclusion

Kachchi and pakki parchi split a cloth deal into two steps: check, then settle. Keep that order whatever paperwork you use. Agree the specification, measure and inspect, and settle only on checked figures.
