Viscose, modal and lyocell are all regenerated cellulose fibres: they start as wood pulp, are dissolved, and are spun back into fibre. They are often described as if they were the same material. They are not: the way the cellulose is dissolved differs, and so do the fibres' properties and environmental profile. This guide explains the differences using the producer technical information published by Lenzing, and explains where viscose fits in polyester-viscose fabric.

What regenerated cellulose means

Cotton is a natural cellulose fibre grown as a fibre. Regenerated cellulose fibres begin as cellulose from another source, usually wood-based dissolving pulp, which is chemically dissolved and then spun through fine holes into a bath to form new continuous filaments. Because the fibre is manufactured, its length, fineness and properties can be controlled to a degree that natural fibres cannot.

How viscose is made

According to Lenzing’s technical description, viscose production starts with dissolving pulp steeped in sodium hydroxide (lye) to make alkali cellulose. The alkali cellulose then undergoes xanthation with carbon disulfide and dissolves in lye as a deep orange, highly viscous solution. This solution is spun into an acidic bath, and the filaments are stretched, cut, desulfurised, washed and finished (Lenzing, Fiber technologies). The key point is the chemical derivatisation step: cellulose is reacted before it dissolves.

How modal differs

Lenzing describes modal as following a similar path to viscose, with modified spinning-solution and process settings that give the fibre high wet strength. The source gives no numerical strengths, so this article does not quote any.

How lyocell is made

Lyocell skips xanthation. Cellulose is dissolved directly in an organic solvent, N-methylmorpholine-N-oxide (NMMO); the resulting dope is filtered and wet-spun. Lenzing states that approximately 99.8% of the NMMO is recovered and reused in its process, and that the raw material can be wood-based cellulose or alternative sources such as cotton scraps (Lenzing). That recovery figure is a producer’s statement about its own process and is not a statement about every lyocell mill.

Viscose in PV fabric

The “V” in PV is viscose. Viscose is blended with polyester because the two fibres contribute different things: polyester adds strength and shape retention, and viscose brings the feel and moisture behaviour of a cellulosic fibre. Moisture regain, a measure of how much water a fibre absorbs from the air, differs sharply between polyester and cellulosic fibres; see fibre moisture regain and fabric comfort. The two fibres are also dyed with different dye classes (dyeing polyester-viscose blends), and the general behaviour of the blend is covered in the PV blend guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is viscose a natural or synthetic fibre?

It is neither strictly: it is a regenerated cellulose fibre, made from natural cellulose by a chemical process.

What is the difference between viscose and modal?

Modal follows a path similar to viscose with modified settings that give it high wet strength.

What solvent is used to make lyocell?

NMMO (N-methylmorpholine-N-oxide), which dissolves cellulose directly without xanthation.

Is viscose the same as rayon?

The two terms are often used interchangeably in trade; rayon is the wider term for regenerated cellulose fibres, and viscose names the process.

Why is viscose blended with polyester?

To combine polyester’s strength and shape retention with the feel and moisture behaviour of a cellulosic fibre.

Conclusion

Viscose, modal and lyocell share a raw material but not a process. Viscose and modal reach solution through xanthation; lyocell dissolves cellulose directly. When a specification says “viscose”, it should say which fibre and which blend ratio. Claims about performance or environmental profile should be tied to a named producer and process.
