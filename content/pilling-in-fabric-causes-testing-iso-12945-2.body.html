Pilling is the small balls of fibre that appear on a fabric surface after wear and washing. It is one of the most common complaints about blended fabrics, and it is measured in the laboratory rather than judged by eye alone. This guide explains how pills form, what raises or lowers the risk, how the Martindale test (ISO 12945-2) and the random tumble test (ASTM D3512) work, and what a buyer should ask for.

How pills form

A research paper on polyester-cotton blends from the University of Management and Technology, Lahore describes pilling as a three-stage process (Hussain and Khan, Journal of Design and Textiles, 2024):

  1. Fuzz formation. Rubbing pulls loose fibre ends to the fabric surface and forms a layer of fluff.
  2. Entanglement. Continued rubbing tangles the loose fibres into small balls (pills).
  3. Growth and wear-off. Each pill is held to the fabric by one or more anchor fibres. The paper says these are typically polyester fibres that have partly worked out of the spun yarn. Whether a fabric looks pilled depends on the balance between pill formation and pill break-off. If formation outpaces break-off, the fabric pills. If break-off keeps pace, pills do not accumulate. Over long rubbing the supply of free fibre runs out and the number of pills falls.

This explains an important point for blended cloth. In a fabric made only of weak fibres, pills tend to break away on their own. A strong fibre such as polyester can hold the pill on the surface for longer, which is why the fibre mix and the yarn construction matter more than the blend label alone.

What affects pilling

The same paper lists the factors that increase or decrease pilling. They are findings reported in that paper; individual results vary with the specific fabric.

LevelAssociated with more pilling
FibreFiner synthetic fibre (below about 1.5 denier per filament, against 2.5 or more); higher fibre strength; short fibre length; a smoother, lower-friction fibre surface
YarnLow twist; hairy yarn; open-end spinning compared with ring spinning; mixing fibres of different length or fineness
FabricLoose structure compared with a tight one
ProcessingDyeing and finishing conditions can make pilling worse

How pilling is tested

Martindale method: ISO 12945-2

ISO 12945-2 is titled Textiles — Determination of fabric propensity to surface pilling, fuzzing or matting — Part 2: Modified Martindale method. It specifies a method for determining the resistance of fabrics to pilling, fuzzing and matting (ISO 12945-2:2020 catalogue entry). In outline:

  • A circular specimen is rubbed against a friction surface (the same fabric, or a wool abradant where relevant) under a defined force.
  • The specimen moves in a Lissajous pattern. One “pilling rub” is one revolution of the two outer drives of the machine; a full Lissajous figure is 16 revolutions.
  • After defined numbers of rubs, the surface is assessed visually, following ISO 12945-4 (assessment of pilling, fuzzing and matting by visual analysis).

The catalogue page does not publish the rub counts or the rating scale; those are in the full standard, which a buyer should read or have the laboratory report against.

Random tumble: ASTM D3512

The Lahore paper tested samples with the random tumble pilling test, ASTM D3512: samples are tumbled for 30 minutes and then rated visually on a 1 to 5 scale, where 5 means no pilling and 1 very severe pilling.

Why the method matters

Different methods use different abrasion and rating rules, so a “4” from one method is not a “4” from another. A pilling result is only meaningful when the report states the standard, the number of rubs or minutes, and the rating scale.

What buyers should ask for

  • The test method (for example ISO 12945-2 or ASTM D3512) written into the specification, not just “pilling resistance”.
  • The number of rubs or test time and the minimum rating required.
  • Whether the result is for the bulk lot or an earlier sample. Spinning, finishing and dyeing can all shift pilling between lots.
  • A test report from a named laboratory for the exact article.

Reducing pilling

The Lahore study compared a plasma treatment, a chemical anti-pilling finish and an untreated polyester-cotton sample. The plasma-treated sample resisted pilling better than the other two, and the authors note that chemical anti-pilling finishes wear out with laundering while plasma effects are described as more permanent. This is the result of one small study on one blend, not a general rule. In practice, pilling is managed first through yarn and fabric construction (twist, fibre selection, tighter structure) and then through finishing.

Frequently asked questions

Why does fabric pill?

Rubbing pulls loose fibres to the surface, they tangle into balls, and anchor fibres hold the balls on the fabric.

Does pilling mean the fabric is poor quality?

Not by itself. Pilling depends on fibre, yarn, construction and finishing, and is measured against a stated standard and rating.

What is the Martindale pilling test?

ISO 12945-2: a specimen is rubbed against a friction surface in a Lissajous pattern and the surface is then assessed visually.

What does a pilling rating of 5 mean?

On the ASTM D3512 1–5 scale used in the cited paper, 5 means no pilling and 1 very severe pilling.

Can pilling be removed?

Pills can be cut off, but new ones can form while loose fibre remains. Prevention in the fibre, yarn and fabric is more effective.

Conclusion

Pilling is the result of loose fibre, rubbing and fibres strong enough to anchor pills. It is controlled at the fibre, yarn and fabric stages and verified by a named test with a stated rating scale. Write the method and minimum result into the specification.
