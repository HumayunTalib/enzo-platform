“Wash and wear” is one of the most used names in Pakistan’s men’s suiting trade, and one of the least precise. It tells you what the cloth is meant to do, which is wash, dry and look presentable with little or no ironing. It does not tell you how the cloth is built. This guide explains where the term comes from, which blends usually sit behind it, how to read a construction such as 112 × 84, and how to judge a wash and wear fabric in hand before you buy in bulk.

What “wash and wear” means

The term is older than most of the fabrics sold under it. It came into use in the 1950s, when synthetic fabrics and treated cotton were promoted as clothes that could be washed and worn without ironing. Even then the claim was partly marketing, because light touch-up ironing was often still needed (Wikipedia, Wrinkle-resistant fabric). Related labels such as “easy care”, “non-iron” and “durable press” describe the same goal, with different levels of performance.

In Pakistan’s unstitched men’s trade, wash and wear is sold as a practical, low-maintenance shalwar kameez fabric. One large retailer’s buying guide describes it as fabric “treated to resist wrinkling” that “requires minimal ironing” (Alkaram Studio fabric guide). That is a description of behaviour. It is not a fibre content, a weight or a construction. Two cloths sold as “wash n wear” can differ in all three.

Typical blends behind the name

Crease resistance comes from two places: the fibre and the finish. Synthetic fibres such as polyester absorb little water and resist wrinkling by nature. Cellulosic fibres such as cotton, viscose (rayon) and linen crease easily, so cellulose-rich cloths need a crease-resist finish. That finish is usually a resin treatment that cross-links the cellulose chains so they cannot move as freely when wet or stressed (Wikipedia).

This is why wash and wear suiting is normally a polyester blend. The two common families are:

  • Polyester-viscose (PV). Polyester gives crease recovery and strength; viscose gives a softer hand, better moisture absorption and a fuller drape. See our PV blend guide.
  • Polyester-cotton (PC or TC). Cotton adds a drier, more natural feel. The comparison is covered in polyester-viscose vs polyester-cotton.

The blend ratio matters as much as the fibres. A polyester-rich blend will usually recover from creasing better than a viscose-rich or cotton-rich one, but it may feel less cool in summer. If a supplier’s abbreviations are unclear, our note on PV, TR, TC and CVC explains them. Always ask for the blend in writing as percentages.

Reading reed × pick: 112 × 84 as a worked example

A construction such as 112 × 84 gives two thread densities. The first number is the reed, the warp ends per inch running along the length of the cloth. The second is the pick, the weft threads per inch running across it. Our guide to reed and pick explains how both are counted with a pick glass.

For 112 × 84, a one-inch square of fabric holds 112 warp threads and 84 weft threads. Three things follow:

  • The cloth is warp-dense. The ratio is 4 to 3, so there are a third more threads in the length than in the width. The balance between the two directions affects how the cloth drapes, how firm it feels and how it looks on the face.
  • The numbers alone do not give the weight. GSM depends on the yarn count as well as the thread density. The same 112 × 84 woven with finer yarn will be lighter than one woven with coarser yarn. Our GSM from EPI and PPI guide shows the calculation.
  • The stage matters. Finishing can shrink or stretch the cloth, which moves the count. Ask whether a construction is quoted on greige (loom-state) or finished fabric.

Higher numbers mean denser, not automatically better. A construction should match the purpose: a medium-weight suiting for daily and occasion wear needs enough density to hold shape without feeling stiff.

Judging quality in hand

You do not need a laboratory to screen a sample, though a laboratory confirms what your hands suggest.

  • Crease recovery. Crush a corner of the cloth in your fist for a short while, release it and watch how quickly and how fully the creases drop out. Compare samples side by side under the same conditions. The formal version of this check is the crease recovery angle test in ISO 2313-1:2021, which measures how far a folded specimen opens after a load is removed. The standard warns that results for very different kinds of fabric cannot be compared directly.
  • Weight. A heavier cloth is not always better, but weight should match what you were quoted. Cut a GSM sample with a round cutter and weigh it, or ask for the figure in writing. See what GSM means.
  • Shade consistency. Check the shade of different pieces, and of the start, middle and end of a single piece, in daylight. A shade card is only useful if bulk matches it.
  • Surface and handle. Rub the face lightly and look for fuzz or early pilling. Our note on pilling and ISO 12945-2 covers the test.
  • Width. Measure edge to edge. Width decides how many suits a piece yields; see fabric width and garment consumption.

Greige or finished wash and wear

Wash and wear is sold at two stages. Greige is the cloth as it comes off the loom, before dyeing and finishing. Processors, converters and exporters buy it to dye and finish to their own shade and handle. Finished fabric has been dyed and finished and is ready to cut and stitch. Wholesalers, shops and stitching units usually buy at this stage.

Much of what a customer feels as “wash and wear” is settled in finishing: the dye shade, the handle and, for cellulose-rich blends, the crease-resist treatment. Greige buyers should therefore judge the construction and yarn quality, then plan the finishing route. Finished-fabric buyers should judge the end result. Our greige vs finished fabric guide goes into the trade-offs.

What to ask a supplier

  1. Blend, as percentages, and fibre types.
  2. Construction (reed × pick), and whether it is the greige or finished count.
  3. Warp and weft yarn counts. See what yarn count means.
  4. GSM and width, with tolerances, in writing.
  5. Shade card, and whether bulk will be checked against it.
  6. How pieces are inspected before dispatch.

Frequently asked questions

Is wash and wear a fibre?

No. It is a performance name for fabric that needs little or no ironing after washing. The fibre content, usually a polyester blend, has to be stated separately.

What is the difference between wash and wear and wash n wear?

None. “Wash n wear” is a common trade spelling of the same term.

Does 112 × 84 tell me the GSM?

No. It gives thread density. GSM also depends on the warp and weft yarn counts and on finishing.

Does 100% cotton count as wash and wear?

Cotton creases easily by nature. It can be given a crease-resist resin finish, but most fabrics sold as wash and wear are polyester blends.

How can I compare crease recovery between two samples?

Crush both in the same way for the same time and compare how they recover. For a formal comparison, ask a laboratory for a crease recovery angle test under ISO 2313-1.

Conclusion

Wash and wear describes behaviour, not build. To know what you are buying, ask for the blend, the reed × pick, the yarn counts, the GSM and the width in writing. Then test crease recovery and shade in hand before committing to bulk.
