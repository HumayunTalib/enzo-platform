A polyester-viscose (PV) fabric contains two fibres that cannot be dyed with the same dye. Polyester takes disperse dyes at high temperature; viscose, a cellulosic fibre, takes dyes such as reactive dyes under alkaline conditions. How the two stages are combined affects shade, fastness, cost and water use. This guide explains the logic using published research and technical references, and what it means when you buy dyed blended fabric.

Why two dye classes

Polyester is hydrophobic and cotton or viscose is hydrophilic. Disperse dyes suit polyester, while reactive dyes suit cellulosic fibres. A blend therefore has to be dyed with a disperse dye for the polyester and a reactive (or other cellulosic) dye for the viscose; a technical reference on polyester-cotton blends states exactly this pairing and calls the two-bath process the more suitable route to the optimum shade (Textile Learner). The principle is the same for polyester-viscose, because viscose is also a cellulosic fibre. For what PV is and how it differs from polyester-cotton, see the PV blend guide and PV versus PC.

Dyeing the polyester component

Two conditions are reported in the sources reviewed.

  • Exhaust dyeing. Polyester is dyed under high temperature and pressure. A reference lists jet machines operating up to 140 °C, with the bath raised to about 130 °C, at a mildly acidic pH of about 4 to 6 (Textile Learner). A 2024 study of a cotton/polyester fabric also dyed the polyester at 130 °C for 30 minutes at pH 4–5 (Elamri et al., Indian Journal of Fibre & Textile Research, 2024).
  • Continuous (pad-thermofix). The fabric is padded with disperse dye and heat-fixed. In a 2015 study on a 48% polyester / 52% viscose fabric, the polyester was padded to 70% expression and thermofixed at 210 °C for one minute (Borse and Dorugade, International Journal on Textile Engineering and Processes, 2015).

After disperse dyeing, loose dye on the surface is usually removed by reduction clearing. The same PV study cleared with sodium hydrosulphite and sodium hydroxide at 60 °C for 10 minutes, then washed and neutralised. Reduction clearing improves wash fastness; it is also the step that one-bath methods try to reduce or remove.

Dyeing the viscose component

Reactive dyes bond chemically to cellulose under alkaline conditions. In the 2015 PV study the viscose was dyed by cold pad-batch: padding with reactive dye, soda ash (20 g/l) and sodium hydroxide, then holding the fabric 12 to 16 hours for fixation before washing and soaping at 95 °C.

The study varied the added caustic soda (1, 2 and 4 g/l) over five shades and found that colour strength generally rose with more alkali. The effect was larger for darker shades: the deepest shade tested gained about 18% in colour strength at 4 g/l compared with soda ash alone. The authors caution that alkali above the optimum can increase dye hydrolysis and reduce the result. One result in the data (the lightest shade at 1 g/l) fell slightly below the control and was not explained. This is a single laboratory study of one fabric and one set of dyes, so the numbers show a direction, not a rule.

Two-bath, one-bath and continuous routes

RouteHow it worksReported trade-off
Two-bath, two-stepPolyester dyed and reduction-cleared; then the cellulosic fibre dyed in a separate bathIn the 2024 cotton/polyester study this gave high colour strength but used the most water, energy and chemicals
One-bath, two-stepPolyester dyed, then reactive dye added to the same bath without drainingSame study: lower reactive exhaustion (about 67% against about 82%), colour strength still within acceptable limits, savings in water, energy and time
One-bath, one-stepBoth dyes in one bath with a carrierSame study: lowest exhaustion (about 70%), and no reactive dye fixed on the polyester face; removes the sodium hydrosulphite step
Continuous pad (pad-thermofix + pad-batch)Polyester padded and heat-fixed, then reactive dye padded and batchedUsed in the 2015 PV study

A separate paper on polyester/viscose blends describes a one-bath, two-stage heating process in which alkali is added for the reactive stage and acetic acid for the disperse stage; the acid and alkali neutralise each other and form a sodium acetate buffer. In a factory trial the authors reported colour strength and fastness equal to the traditional process, with shorter time and lower water and alkali use (Advanced Materials Research, vol. 441; only the abstract was reviewed).

What this means for buyers

  • Shade is lot-specific. Each dye lot is a separate dyeing run, so shade can differ slightly between lots. Approve a lab dip or sample, and check pieces against it (fabric inspection checklist).
  • Ask for colour fastness results for the specific article, with the method named, instead of accepting a general statement.
  • Dye choice and process route affect cost and fastness, which can contribute to price differences between PV fabrics (why fabric prices differ).

Frequently asked questions

Why can't one dye colour a PV fabric?

Polyester and viscose are chemically different. Disperse dyes colour polyester; reactive dyes colour cellulosic fibres such as viscose. A blend needs both.

At what temperature is polyester dyed?

Sources reviewed report about 130 °C in exhaust dyeing (jets up to 140 °C) and thermofixation at about 210 °C in continuous dyeing.

What is reduction clearing?

A wash with a reducing agent after disperse dyeing to remove unfixed surface dye and improve wash fastness.

Is one-bath dyeing as good as two-bath?

Studies report comparable colour strength in some cases, with savings in water, energy and time, and lower exhaustion in others. Results depend on dyes and conditions.

Why do two lots of the same colour look different?

Each lot is a separate dye run. Small differences in dye, liquor, temperature or fabric can shift shade.

Conclusion

Dyeing a PV blend is two dyeing jobs on one fabric: disperse dye for polyester at high temperature, and reactive or other cellulosic dye for viscose under alkaline conditions. The route chosen changes cost, time, water use and sometimes fastness. For a buyer, the practical answer is to approve shade against a sample, state fastness tests in the specification, and expect small lot-to-lot variation.
