Not every buyer needs a full greige lot. A stitching unit filling a few hundred orders, a shop restocking three shades, or a small brand testing a new line usually wants cloth that is already dyed, finished and ready to cut, in quantities it can sell through. This guide explains when buying finished fabric in small batches makes sense, how it differs from bulk greige, and what to ask before you order.

What small-batch finished fabric means

Finished fabric has been dyed and finished after weaving. It is ready to cut and stitch. Greige is cloth as it comes off the loom, before any wet processing. Most of what reaches retail markets is already finished. Dawn’s reporting on Karachi’s Kapra Market, for example, found that “mostly finished and dyed fabrics” were arriving there, with grey cloth only a small share (Dawn, 21 February 2021).

A small batch simply means buying finished cloth in a quantity sized to your own sales, rather than the full lot a processor or converter would buy. What counts as small differs between suppliers, constructions and shades, so always ask for the supplier’s minimum in writing.

The trade-off is control versus convenience. A greige buyer controls the shade and finish but needs a processor, working capital and enough volume to justify a processing run. A finished-fabric buyer gives up some control in return for cloth that can go straight to the cutting table. When small batches make sense

  • You have no processor. If you cannot dye and finish greige yourself, finished fabric removes a whole stage of the supply chain.
  • Your demand is spread across shades. A shop that sells a little of many colours gains more from range than from depth.
  • You are testing a line. A small brand can order a first batch, see what sells, and reorder the winners.
  • Cash and storage are tight. Smaller orders tie up less money and less space.
  • You need cloth quickly for a season. Finished stock avoids processing time on your side, though you still need to ask about the supplier’s timing. Our Eid stocking calendar explains how to plan around 15 Shaban.

Small batches make less sense when you need one shade in large volume, when you need a custom shade or finish, or when you have your own processing arrangements. Then bulk greige usually gives better control.

What to ask before ordering

A short, written request saves most problems later.

  1. Shade. Ask for a physical shade card or swatch, not just a photo. Screens change colours. Ask whether bulk will come from a single dye lot or several.
  2. Width. Ask for the usable width, edge to edge, excluding selvedge. Width decides how many garments or suit lengths you get from each metre; see fabric width and garment consumption.
  3. Metres per piece. Ask how long each piece or roll is, so you can plan your cutting and avoid awkward short ends. Our guide to fabric consumption and yield helps here.
  4. Weight and construction. Ask for GSM and reed × pick. See what GSM means and reed and pick.
  5. Blend. Ask for fibre percentages in writing.
  6. Sample. Ask for a cut sample large enough to wash, iron and stitch a test piece.
  7. Minimum and reorder. Ask for the minimum order per shade and whether the same shade can be reordered later.
  8. Inspection. Ask how cloth is inspected before dispatch.

Checking the cloth when it arrives

Even with a good supplier, check before you cut:

  • Count pieces and measure metres on a sample of them.
  • Measure width at the start, middle and end of a piece.
  • Compare shade against the approved swatch in daylight, and compare pieces against each other.
  • Look for faults across the full width.
  • Wash a small sample and measure shrinkage before cutting a full lay.

Common mistakes

  • Approving from a photo. Always see the cloth.
  • Mixing dye lots in one order. Garments cut from different lots can show shade differences side by side.
  • Ignoring width. A cheaper rate on narrower cloth can cost more per garment. Our GSM, width and cost guide shows the arithmetic.
  • Skipping the wash test. Shrinkage found after stitching is expensive.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between greige and finished fabric?

Greige is cloth straight off the loom, before dyeing and finishing. Finished fabric has been dyed and finished and is ready to cut.

Who should buy small-batch finished fabric?

Stitching units, shops and smaller brands that have no processor, need a range of shades, or want to test demand before ordering more.

What is a typical minimum for small-batch fabric?

It differs by supplier, construction and shade. Ask each supplier for its minimum per shade in writing.

How do I make sure the shade matches across my order?

Approve a physical swatch, ask whether the order comes from one dye lot, and check pieces against each other on arrival.

Why does width matter so much?

Width decides how many garments you cut from each metre, so it directly changes your cost per garment.

Conclusion

Small-batch finished fabric trades some control for speed and flexibility. It suits buyers who need range, have no processor or want to test demand. Ask for shade, width, metres per piece, weight, blend and a sample in writing, and check every delivery before you cut.
