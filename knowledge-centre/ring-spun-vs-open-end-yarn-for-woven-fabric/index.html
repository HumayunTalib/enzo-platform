Ring-Spun vs Open-End (Rotor) Yarn: What Research Shows for Woven Fabric Buyers

By ENZO Textiles · Published 19 November 2026 · 2 min read

In this article
  1. How the two methods differ
  2. What one study measured
  3. Why it matters in weaving
  4. What to ask a supplier

Ring spinning and rotor (open-end) spinning are the two dominant ways to make staple yarn for woven fabric. They differ in how fibres are assembled and twisted, and that changes yarn strength, evenness and hairiness. This article summarises one published comparison and lists what to ask a supplier before you specify either.

DefinitionRing spinning drafts a fibre strand and twists it with a traveller running on a ring, then winds it onto a bobbin. Rotor (open-end) spinning feeds a sliver into a rapidly rotating rotor where individual fibres are collected and twisted into a yarn.

How the two methods differ

In ring spinning the fibres stay in a continuous strand from drafting to winding, and twist is inserted along that strand. In rotor spinning the sliver is broken into individual fibres, which are carried into the rotor groove and twisted into a yarn as it is drawn out. Rotor spinning is a higher-throughput process, and the paper cited below puts rotor yarn at around 20% of staple-yarn production.

What one study measured

Islam (2019), published in the European Scientific Journal, compared ring-spun and rotor yarns at yarn level. In the samples tested:

PropertyRing-spunRotor (open-end)
Count-strength product (CSP)About 2,300About 1,700 (roughly a quarter lower)
HairinessHigherAbout 12% lower
EvennessLess evenMore even

Read this as one data point, not a rule. The numbers come from the yarns and settings in that study; fibre, count, twist and machine condition all shift them. The paper measures yarn, not woven fabric, so it does not tell you how a fabric will behave in use. Check the paper for the fibre and count tested before comparing with your blend.

Why it matters in weaving

Warp yarns are under more tension and abrasion on the loom than weft yarns, so warp strength and evenness matter for weaving efficiency. Hairiness affects how the surface looks and feels and how much fibre sheds during preparation. A buyer who cares about surface and handle will want hairiness and evenness in the conversation; one who cares about robustness will ask about strength.

What to ask a supplier

  • Is the warp ring-spun or rotor? Is the weft the same?
  • What is the count, in Ne, for each (see yarn count systems)?
  • Is there test data for strength and evenness on the lot being used?
  • Is the fabric specification written around the spinning method, or does the supplier choose?

ENZO procures yarn independently to the specification of each order, so the spinning system for warp and weft should be stated in your RFQ if it matters to you.

Frequently asked questions

Is ring-spun yarn stronger than open-end yarn?

In the study cited here, ring-spun yarn had a higher count-strength product (about 2,300 against about 1,700 for rotor yarn). Results depend on fibre, count and machine settings.

Which yarn is hairier?

In the same study, rotor yarn was about 12% less hairy than ring-spun yarn.

Can I assume the same result for a PV blend?

No. Test data for your fibre blend and count is needed before assuming the same difference.
