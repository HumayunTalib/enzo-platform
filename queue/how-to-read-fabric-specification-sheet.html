How to Read (and Write) a Fabric Specification Sheet: Every Line Explained

By ENZO Textiles · Published {{PUB_DATE_H}} · 4 min read

A fabric specification sheet is the document that makes an order quotable and enforceable. It describes the cloth precisely enough that any mill can price it, any laboratory can test it and any inspector can accept or reject it. Most disputes in bulk fabric trade trace back to a specification that left a line vague. This guide walks through each line of a woven fabric specification, what it means and what to write. How constructions are written

Published trade specifications write a woven construction as: warp count and blend, weft count and blend, ends × picks per inch, and weave. A sample PV 65/35 sheet shows entries such as a 2/40 PV warp with a 20s PV weft at 84 × 64 in a 2/1 twill, alongside plain-weave options at 72 × 56 (Fibre2Fashion). Reading left to right: yarn count/ply and fibre for the warp, then for the weft, then EPI × PPI, then the weave.

Tolerances: the lines most often left out

A specification without tolerances is a wish. Every measured line needs a limit: composition, GSM, width, shade, shrinkage and quantity. Tolerances are agreed between buyer and seller; there is no universal figure. A sensible approach is to set the tightest limit you genuinely need, because tighter limits cost more to meet.

Frequently asked questions

What is on a fabric specification sheet?

Composition, yarn count, construction (EPI × PPI), weave, weight, width, finish, shade, test requirements, inspection method and commercial terms, each with a tolerance where it can be measured.

What does 84 × 64 mean on a fabric spec?

84 ends per inch in the warp direction and 64 picks per inch in the weft direction.

What does 2/40 mean?

A two-ply yarn of 40s count, which behaves as an effective count of 20.

Do I need to know everything to request a quote?

No. Fill in what you know and leave the rest to your supplier, who can propose a construction. The more you state, the less is left to interpretation.

Should the specification be loom-state or finished?

State which. Width, thread count and weight all change in finishing, so the two sets of numbers differ.

Conclusion

A specification is a contract in miniature. Fill every line you can, add a tolerance to everything measurable, and name the standard behind every test.
