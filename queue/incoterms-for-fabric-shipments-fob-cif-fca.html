Incoterms for Fabric Shipments: FOB, CIF, FCA and Which Fits a Container of Rolls

By ENZO Textiles · Published {{PUB_DATE_H}} · 4 min read

Every export contract for fabric needs a delivery term. Incoterms 2020, published by the International Chamber of Commerce, are the standard set of three-letter rules that say where the seller’s job ends, who pays for freight and insurance, and who carries the risk of loss or damage in transit. Choosing the wrong term for the way your fabric travels is a common and avoidable mistake. This guide compares the terms buyers meet most often and how to write them correctly.

What an Incoterm does and does not do

Incoterms define delivery points, the transfer of risk and the division of costs, and obligations such as export and import clearance (UPS). They do not cover payment terms, the transfer of ownership or the contract’s other conditions. There are eleven rules: seven work for any mode of transport (EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP) and four are for sea and inland waterway transport only (FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF) (Xflow).

FOB vs CIF: the comparison everyone asks about

Under both FOB and CIF, risk passes to the buyer when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of origin. The difference is cost and control: under FOB the buyer arranges and pays ocean freight and insurance, while under CIF the seller includes freight and minimum insurance to the destination port in its price (Xflow). CIF is convenient, but you rely on the seller’s freight arrangement and a minimum insurance cover; FOB gives you control of carrier choice and freight cost (Cargolinked).

Why containers favour FCA, CPT or CIP

FOB, CFR and CIF assume the goods are placed on board a vessel by the seller. A container is normally handed to a carrier at a terminal long before it is lifted aboard, so the sea-only rules put the transfer of risk at the wrong moment. The ICC guidance, as summarised by freight sources, is to use FCA, CPT or CIP for containerised cargo (Xflow; Cargolinked). Rolls of fabric usually travel in containers, so FCA with a named place is often the better fit, even though FOB is more commonly quoted.

Insurance: a 2020 change to note

Only CIF and CIP oblige the seller to buy insurance. Under Incoterms 2020, CIF still requires only minimum cover (Institute Cargo Clauses C) while CIP requires broader cover (Institute Cargo Clauses A) (Cargolinked). For fabric, which can be damaged by water or handling, check what cover applies and consider arranging your own policy if the seller’s is minimal.

How to write the term in your order

  1. Write the rule, the named place or port and the year: for example “FCA Karachi Incoterms 2020”. Leaving out the year can cause disputes (ICC introduction).
  2. Match the term to the transport mode.
  3. State who handles export clearance and who handles import clearance and duties.
  4. Agree packing and marking, which determine whether rolls arrive in good condition; see the specification sheet guide.
  5. Check the HS code you will declare; see HS code for polyester viscose fabric.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between FOB and CIF?

Risk passes at the same point (on board at origin). Under FOB the buyer arranges freight and insurance; under CIF the seller includes freight and minimum insurance to the destination port in its price.

Which Incoterm is best for containers?

ICC guidance favours FCA, CPT or CIP for containers, because FOB, CFR and CIF assume loading onto a vessel.

Does CIF include insurance?

Yes, but only minimum cover (Institute Cargo Clauses C). CIP requires broader cover (Clauses A).

How do I write an Incoterm?

State the rule, the named place and the year, for example “FCA Karachi Incoterms 2020”.

Is FCA safer than EXW for buyers?

Under FCA the seller clears the goods for export, which is why many buyers prefer it to EXW.

Conclusion

Choose the term that matches how the fabric actually travels, name the place and year, and decide who covers insurance. That one line in the order settles most arguments about damaged or delayed cloth.
