Buying Guides

Fabric Sampling Before a Bulk Order: Swatch, Lab Dip, Handloom, Strike-Off and Bulk Approval

By ENZO Textiles · Published {{PUB_DATE_H}} · 4 min read

Sampling is how a buyer and a mill agree on what “this fabric” means before the money is committed. Different samples answer different questions, so the order and the paperwork matter. Skipping a stage, or approving a colour with no written tolerance, is a common cause of bulk disputes. This guide explains the stages, what each confirms and what to write down.

The stages and what each confirms

SampleForConfirms
Swatch / hangerAny fabricLook, handfeel, rough weight; a starting point for discussion
Lab dipPlain-dyed (solid) fabricColour match against your standard on the actual fibre
HandloomYarn-dyed fabric (stripes, checks)Colour and pattern of the yarn-dyed design
Strike-offPrinted fabricPrint colour, placement, scale and repeat
Bulk / production sampleAny fabricThat bulk cloth matches the approved sample before cutting

Terminology follows common usage: for plain-dyed fabrics the sample is called a lab dip, and for yarn-dyed fabrics such as stripes and checks it is called a handloom (Wikipedia). A lab dip is a colour-approval swatch dyed to match a target on a specific fibre and dye system, while a strike-off reproduces a printed design on the production fabric (Racklify).

A sensible sequence

  1. Confirm the specification first. Composition, construction and weight come before colour; see how to read a fabric specification sheet.
  2. Request swatches of the base cloth to check look and handfeel.
  3. Submit your colour standard (a physical swatch, a Pantone TCX reference, or both) and approve a lab dip or handloom.
  4. Test the sample for the properties that matter, such as shrinkage and colour fastness, under the standards in fabric quality tests.
  5. Approve in writing with a stated colour tolerance and the date.
  6. Confirm the bulk fabric against the approved sample before cutting or shipping.

Why a written tolerance matters

Approving a lab dip without a defined tolerance is, in one manufacturer’s words, a handshake rather than an approval: the tolerance defines how far bulk may deviate before it counts as a rejection (Runtang). Bulk colour can also drift after approval, for reasons such as variation in the base cloth lot, so many buyers treat bulk colour approval as a separate gate from lab-dip approval (Runtang Textile).

A note on blended fabrics

Blends such as polyester–viscose are dyed with two dye systems, one for each fibre (see the PV fabric guide and the dyeing work on PV blends in NMIMS). A lab dip on a blend is therefore worth testing under different light sources and after washing, not only judged by eye.

What to write down

  • The standard: physical swatch or code, and the lighting conditions for comparison.
  • The colour tolerance for bulk against the approved sample.
  • Who approves, and how (a signed form, or an email with the sample reference).
  • How many samples are included and who pays for extra rounds.
  • The timetable: sample dispatch, approval deadline, bulk start.

Frequently asked questions

What is a lab dip in fabric?

A small dyed swatch made to match a colour standard on a specific fibre, submitted for approval before bulk dyeing.

What is the difference between a lab dip and a strike-off?

A lab dip approves a solid colour; a strike-off approves a printed design, including placement and repeat.

What is a handloom sample?

The yarn-dyed equivalent of a lab dip, used for stripes and checks.

Is lab dip approval enough?

It is the first gate. Confirm the bulk fabric against the approved sample before cutting, with a written colour tolerance.

How long does sampling take?

It depends on the mill and the number of rounds. Ask for a timetable and agree approval deadlines in the order.

Conclusion

Match each sample to the question it answers, approve in writing with a tolerance, and check the bulk against the approved sample before it is cut. That sequence is what makes the second order the same as the first.
