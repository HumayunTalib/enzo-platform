Common Greige Fabric Defects and How to Spot Them

Cluster: Quality Control · Primary keyword: greige fabric defects

Catching a defect at the greige stage is cheaper than catching it later — before dyeing, finishing, and cutting costs have been added on top of a flaw that was there from the start.

Common greige defects

DefectWhat it looks likeTypical cause
Missing endsA thin line or gap running the length of the fabricA warp yarn breaking during weaving and not being repaired
Broken picksA short gap or float in the weft directionWeft yarn breaking mid-insertion
SlubsIrregular thick spots in the yarn, visible on the surfaceUneven yarn spinning
Reed marksFine lines running the length of the fabric at regular intervalsDamaged or misaligned reed dents
Weft barsA visible band running the full width of the fabricLoom stoppage or a change in weft tension/count mid-run
Width variationFabric measuring differently at different points along the rollInconsistent tension control during weaving

How these are checked

Greige inspection is typically done by unrolling fabric over a lit table or frame and checking visually for the defects above, combined with a pick-glass check of EPI/PPI (see reading a fabric's density numbers) and a width check at multiple points along the roll.

Why greige-stage inspection matters more than it seems: a defect caught here costs a roll. The same defect, missed and carried through dyeing, finishing, and cutting, costs the roll plus every processing step already applied to it — see our full inspection checklist for the scoring system used to formalise this.

What happens to a defective roll

Depending on severity, a defect can result in the roll being downgraded (sold at a lower grade for a less demanding application), the affected section being cut out, or full rejection of the roll — the outcome should be agreed contractually before production, not decided ad hoc when a defect is found.

Frequently asked questions

Can greige defects be fixed?

Some, like isolated broken picks, can be hand-repaired. Structural issues like consistent width variation or reed marks generally can't be corrected after weaving.

Do all greige defects carry through to the finished fabric?

Most do — dyeing and finishing don't fix structural weaving flaws, and in some cases (like shade absorption differences at a slub) can make them more visible, not less.
