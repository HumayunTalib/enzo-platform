OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 for Fabric Buyers: What It Certifies, What It Does Not, and How to Check It

By ENZO Textiles · Published 27 November 2026 · 4 min read

OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 is one of the most requested documents in textile buying, particularly by buyers selling into Europe and North America. It is a product label showing that a textile has been tested for harmful substances. It is not an organic label, not a sustainability audit, and not a guarantee about a mill’s entire output. Knowing exactly what it covers helps you request the right document and verify it.

What the certification is

OEKO-TEX describes STANDARD 100 as an independent, globally standardised testing and certification system for textiles and accessories tested for harmful substances (OEKO-TEX factsheet). Its approved labelling statements say that each component of a certified product has been laboratory tested against strict limit values for over 1,000 harmful substances (OEKO-TEX labelling guide).

The four product classes

Requirements depend on how the textile is used: the closer to the skin, the stricter the limits. The factsheet distinguishes four product classes: infants and young children, skin contact, without skin contact, and accessory materials (OEKO-TEX factsheet). When you ask a supplier for a certificate, name the class that matches your end use, such as skin contact for garments.

What it does not mean

  • It does not mean organic. STANDARD 100 applies to all textiles, not only natural fibres. Organic cotton has its own, separate certification (Sustainable Jungle).
  • It is not a social or environmental audit. It tests the product for substances; it does not cover working conditions or farming practices.
  • It is not permanent. The certificate is valid for one year (OEKO-TEX factsheet).
  • It is not a general quality grade. It says nothing about shrinkage, strength or colour fastness; for those see fabric quality tests.

How buyers should verify a certificate

  1. Ask for the certificate number, the issuing institute and the validity dates.
  2. Check it online. OEKO-TEX says certificate and label validity can be verified through its Label Check on oeko-tex.com (labelling guide).
  3. Match the scope. A certificate covers the company and the articles described in it. Confirm that the fabric you are buying, in that composition and colour range, is covered.
  4. Check the dates. An expired certificate is not proof for a current order.
  5. Write it into the order if you need it, with the certificate number and class, as a line in your specification.

Questions to ask a supplier

  • Which of your products and colours are covered by a current certificate?
  • Is the certificate in your company’s name or a supplier’s (for example a yarn supplier)?
  • Which product class does it cover?
  • Will the bulk fabric come from the certified process, or are some steps subcontracted?

These belong alongside the broader checks in how to evaluate a Pakistani fabric supplier.

Frequently asked questions

What does OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 certify?

That a textile and its components have been tested for harmful substances against defined limits.

Is OEKO-TEX the same as organic?

No. STANDARD 100 is about harmful substances in the finished product; organic production is covered by separate certifications.

How long is an OEKO-TEX certificate valid?

One year, after which the products must be retested.

How do I verify an OEKO-TEX certificate?

Use the Label Check on the OEKO-TEX website with the certificate number supplied by your seller.

Which product class do I need?

It depends on end use. Garments worn against the skin fall in the skin-contact class; products for infants have the strictest class.

Conclusion

Ask for the certificate number, the class and the dates, verify it online, and make sure it covers the exact fabric you are buying. A certificate is only as useful as its scope.
