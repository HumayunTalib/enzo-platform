Warp vs Weft: The Two Yarn Systems That Build Every Woven Fabric

Cluster: Woven Fabric · Primary keyword: warp vs weft

Every woven fabric is built from two yarn systems running at right angles to each other. Understanding which is which — and why mills often use different yarn for each — explains a surprising amount of what makes one fabric behave differently from another.

Warp: set up first, runs the length of the roll

Warp yarns are threaded onto the loom before weaving begins, running the full length of the fabric (the direction the roll unwinds). Because warp yarns are under continuous tension through the weaving process and have to survive the friction of the loom's moving parts, they're typically the stronger, more tightly twisted yarn in the pair.

Weft: inserted across, pick by pick

Weft yarn is what actually gets woven — inserted across the warp, over-under-over, one pass (one pick) at a time, by a shuttle, rapier, or air/water jet depending on the loom type. Weft doesn't carry the same tension load as warp, so mills often use a slightly different, sometimes less expensive, yarn here without compromising the fabric's overall strength.

Why this matters for cost: because warp and weft frequently use different yarn counts and rates, a fabric's costing sheet always separates the two — see our breakdown of how fabric is priced for the actual formula.

How to tell them apart on a piece of fabric

  • Selvedge. The tightly woven edge that doesn't fray runs parallel to the warp — the selvedge is your fastest visual clue to fabric direction.
  • Stretch. Fabric typically has slightly more give across the weft direction than along the warp, because warp yarn is held under tension during weaving and weft is not.
  • Roll direction. Fabric is rolled along the warp direction — the length of the roll is the warp direction.

Why yarn counts differ between the two

See our explainer on what yarn count means for the full picture, but the short version: warp count and weft count are specified separately on every construction sheet (e.g. "40 warp / 40 weft" or "30 warp / 40 weft") precisely because a mill can — and often does — run a finer or coarser yarn in each direction to hit a specific weight, cost, or hand.

Frequently asked questions

Which is stronger, warp or weft?

Warp is usually the stronger, more tightly twisted yarn, since it has to withstand loom tension throughout weaving. This isn't universal, but it's the common pattern.

Does warp vs weft affect how a garment is cut?

Yes — pattern pieces are typically cut with the warp direction (least stretch) running vertically on the body for stability, though this varies by garment and designer intent.
