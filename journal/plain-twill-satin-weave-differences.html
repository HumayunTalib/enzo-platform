Woven Fabric

Plain, Twill and Satin Weave: How They Differ and Why It Matters

By ENZO Textiles · Published 18 November 2026 · 5 min read

Almost every woven fabric is built from one of three basic weaves: plain, twill or satin (sateen). The weave sets how warp and weft threads cross, and that decides how firm, how smooth, how lustrous and how tightly packed a fabric can be. This guide explains each weave from its interlacing rule, compares them side by side, and shows how weave appears on a fabric specification.

What interlacing means

In woven cloth, lengthwise warp threads (ends) cross widthwise weft threads (picks). Each place where a warp thread passes over or under a weft thread is an interlacing. A float is a stretch where a thread passes over two or more crossing threads without binding. Weaves differ in how often threads interlace and how long the floats are. Many interlacings give a firm, tightly bound cloth with high yarn crimp; long floats give a smoother surface but less binding.

Plain weave

Plain weave is the simplest weave. It repeats on two ends and two picks: each warp thread passes alternately over and under each weft thread, and the next warp thread does the opposite. It needs only two heald shafts on the loom. There are no floats, because every thread is bound on every pick (e-PG Pathshala, Basic weaves).

Because it has the most interlacings of the basic weaves, plain weave gives a firm fabric, and the yarn crimp is higher than in other weaves. Muslin, poplin and cambric are examples of plain-weave cloths. If a specification gives only a construction such as 72 × 56 with no weave named, ask for the weave to be stated.

Twill weave

Twill shows diagonal lines on the fabric surface. The simplest twill repeats on three ends and three picks. Twills are written as fractions such as 2/1, 2/2 or 3/1: the figures give how many threads float over and under at each crossing. The 2/1 twill is the common one in suitings and shirtings (e-PG Pathshala).

  • Warp-faced twill has mostly warp floats on the face, and the back is weft-faced.
  • Weft-faced twill has mostly weft floats on the face.
  • Balanced twill has equal warp and weft floats on both sides.

Twill has fewer interlacings than plain weave, so yarn crimp is lower. The source lists dress materials, suitings, shirtings and bedspreads as examples of twill fabrics, and notes derivatives such as pointed twill, where the diagonal changes direction at set intervals. It gives the diagonal angle as lying between 15° and 75°.

Satin and sateen

In satin and sateen each end and each pick has only one binding point per repeat, and the binding points are spread so that no diagonal line forms. This leaves long floats and a smooth surface. Two numbers define a satin: the repeat size n and a move number. The move number must not be 1 or n−1 (those make twills) and must share no common factor with the repeat size (e-PG Pathshala).

Satin is warp-faced, with warp floats on the face giving the high lustre associated with the weave. Sateen is the weft-faced equivalent. The long floats that give satin its smooth look are also the reason it is more exposed to surface snagging and abrasion than a plain weave, which is why it is usually chosen for appearance rather than hard wear.

The smallest satin repeat (5) and the minimum shaft counts follow from the definitions above: a satin cannot use move 1 or n−1, and n = 5 is the smallest repeat that leaves a valid move number. They are structural facts, not recommendations.

How weave appears on a specification

A construction line normally gives ends × picks and the weave, for example “84 × 64, 2/1 twill”. Because a plain weave binds yarns at every crossing and a twill does not, two fabrics with the same yarn and the same ends × picks but different weaves will not have the same weight, hand or drape. The weave is therefore part of the identity of a fabric and has to be stated. To see how ends and picks are counted, read ends per inch and picks per inch, and for the whole picture see the complete guide to woven fabric.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between plain and twill weave?

Plain weave interlaces warp and weft on every thread, repeating on two ends and two picks. Twill skips threads so that diagonal lines form, with the simplest repeat on three ends and three picks.

What does 2/1 twill mean?

It is the standard notation for a twill in which each crossing floats over two threads and under one (or the reverse on the other face).

What is the difference between satin and sateen?

Satin is warp-faced; sateen is weft-faced. Both use long floats and have no diagonal line.

Which weave has the most interlacings?

Plain weave.

Why does weave matter when comparing fabrics?

The same yarn at the same ends × picks behaves differently in different weaves, so the weave must be stated for a fair comparison.

Conclusion

Plain, twill and satin are three answers to the same question: how often should threads bind? Plain binds most and gives a firm cloth; twill binds less and gives a diagonal; satin binds least and gives a smooth face. Name the weave on every specification.
