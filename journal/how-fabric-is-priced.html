How Fabric Is Priced: Warp, Weft, Yarn and Conversion Cost

Cluster: Costing & Pricing · Primary keyword: how fabric is priced

A fabric quotation looks like one number — rupees per metre. Underneath it is a costing sheet with four or five distinct components, each driven by a different variable. Understanding the breakdown is what lets you judge whether a quote is reasonable, not just whether it's low.

The core components

  • Warp cost. The weight of warp yarn required per metre, multiplied by the warp yarn rate.
  • Weft cost. The weight of weft yarn required per metre, multiplied by the weft yarn rate — calculated separately because warp and weft often use different yarn counts and rates, see our piece on warp vs weft.
  • Conversion cost. The cost of actually weaving the yarn into fabric — driven by machine type and pick density.
  • Finishing cost (kinara/edge finishing and similar). Edge and selvedge treatment costs, which vary by the type of finishing applied.

A simplified, hypothetical worked example

These numbers are illustrative only — not real ENZO pricing — to show how the pieces combine:

Warp weight per metre (lbs) = Total ends ÷ 731 ÷ Warp count Warp cost = Warp weight × Warp yarn rate Weft weight per metre (lbs) = (Picks × Width) ÷ 731 ÷ Weft count Weft cost = Weft weight × Weft yarn rate Conversion cost = Machine rate × Picks Base cost = Warp cost + Weft cost + Conversion cost + Finishing cost

From there, a markup and any applicable brokerage or currency conversion are applied to reach the final quoted rate per metre. The exact markup structure is commercially specific to each mill and isn't something any supplier will publish — what matters for a buyer is understanding which variables move the number, so you can sanity-check a quote when a construction changes.

Why the same construction can cost differently between mills: yarn sourcing rates, machine type, and conversion efficiency all vary between manufacturers even for an identical construction. This is normal and doesn't necessarily indicate a quality difference — see our companion piece on why two similar-looking fabrics can cost differently.

What buyers can actually use this for

You don't need a mill's exact cost sheet to use this usefully. Knowing that warp/weft weight, yarn rate, and pick density are the primary drivers means you can predict, roughly, which direction a quote should move when you change one variable — heavier yarn, higher pick count, or a pricier finishing treatment should all push cost up, and a supplier whose quote doesn't move in the expected direction when you change the spec is worth a follow-up question.

Frequently asked questions

Why are warp and weft costed separately?

Because they frequently use different yarn counts and rates — warp yarn is typically stronger and more tightly twisted to survive loom tension, and is often priced differently from weft yarn.

Will a mill share its exact cost breakdown?

Rarely in full detail — margins and specific supplier rates are commercially sensitive — but a legitimate supplier should be able to explain which construction changes move the price and why.
