Quality Control

Fabric Quality Tests for Bulk Buyers: Shrinkage, Pilling, Strength and Colour Fastness Standards

By ENZO Textiles · Published 4 November 2026 · 6 min read

A fabric specification is only enforceable if it names how each property is measured. “Low shrinkage” and “good colour fastness” are opinions; “dimensional change of no more than ±3% under ISO 5077 and ISO 6330” is a requirement. This guide lists the standard tests a bulk buyer of woven fabric should know, what each one tells you, and how to write them into an order.

Why lab tests, not just inspection

Visual inspection and a pick glass catch defects and construction errors — see how to inspect fabric before placing a bulk order and our inspection checklist. They cannot tell you how a fabric will shrink, pill, fade or break in use. Those properties need standardised lab methods, which is why test standards appear in procurement documents and supplier test reports.

These standards appear in the normative references of national draft standards and in real supplier test reports (DEAS 1141-3; example fabric test report). US-based equivalents also exist, such as AATCC 135 for dimensional change and ASTM D4970 for Martindale pilling, which appear alongside the ISO methods in a Pakistani accredited laboratory’s scope (PNAC).

Shrinkage: dimensional change

Dimensional change is measured by washing and drying a marked specimen and comparing its length and width before and after. ISO 5077 sets out the measurement, ISO 6330 defines the domestic washing and drying procedures, and ISO 3759 covers specimen preparation and marking (Measurlabs). Results are reported separately for warp and weft, with shrinkage as a negative value and extension as positive — a real test report shows, for example, a warp change and a weft change after one wash. Specify the procedure number (wash temperature and cycle) and the number of washes, because results depend on both.

Pilling and abrasion

ISO 12945-2 rubs a fabric specimen under controlled conditions and rates its surface change from 5 (no change) to 1 (severe pilling); a typical rating key describes 4 as slight fuzzing or partly formed pills and 3 as moderate pilling (example test report). Always state the number of rubs: a rating at 500 rubs is not comparable with a rating at 5,000. Blends with a high polyester content merit particular attention here, since polyester is known for weaker pilling performance than cotton (UNIDO). Abrasion resistance (ISO 12947-2) is a separate test that counts rubs until the specimen breaks down.

Tensile and tear strength

ISO 13934-1 measures the breaking force of a woven fabric strip, reported separately for warp and weft, and ISO 13937-3 measures tear strength. In supplier test reports these appear as newtons in each direction. Set minimums by end use and always specify the direction, since warp and weft differ with construction (see ends per inch and picks per inch).

Colour fastness

Colour fastness is rated on a grey-scale of 1 to 5, where 5 is best. The most commonly requested tests for apparel cloth are washing (ISO 105-C06), rubbing (ISO 105-X12) and light (ISO 105-B02). For dyed blends, which are dyed with two dye systems (see the PV fabric guide), ask for fastness results on the finished, dyed cloth and agree the shade approval process in advance.

Setting acceptance thresholds

Standards describe how to test; the pass level is yours to set or to take from a product standard. As an example of how thresholds look, East African draft standards for knitwear require dimensional change of no more than ±5.0% for sweaters and cardigans, ±6% for T-shirts, and a minimum pilling rating of 4 after 500 rubs (DEAS 1141-6; DEAS 1141-3). Those are knitwear figures for a specific market — woven suiting, shirting and workwear call for their own limits, so do not copy them blindly. Use them to see the shape of a requirement, then set values to match your end use, your customer and your destination market.

How to write tests into a purchase order

  1. Name the standard and method (for example, ISO 5077 with ISO 6330 procedure 4N) rather than just the property.
  2. State the conditions: number of washes, rubs, direction (warp/weft) and sample size.
  3. Set the pass limit and the tolerance for each property.
  4. Define when testing happens: pre-production approval, in-line, or final-lot testing, and who pays.
  5. Require a test report from a named laboratory and check the laboratory’s accreditation scope (Pakistan’s accreditation body publishes lab scopes; see the PNAC example above).
  6. Agree a dispute method: a retained sample tested at an agreed third-party lab.

Frequently asked questions

Which test measures fabric shrinkage?

Dimensional change after washing and drying is measured under ISO 5077, using the washing procedures of ISO 6330 and specimen preparation per ISO 3759. AATCC 135 is a widely used alternative method.

What does a pilling rating of 4 mean?

On the standard 1–5 scale used in ISO 12945-2 reports, 4 indicates slight fuzzing or partly formed pills, and 5 indicates no change. Always check the number of rubs the rating refers to.

What is ISO 3801 used for?

ISO 3801 specifies how mass per unit length and mass per unit area of woven fabrics are determined, which is the lab basis for a GSM claim.

What colour fastness tests should I request?

For most apparel fabric: washing (ISO 105-C06), rubbing (ISO 105-X12) and light (ISO 105-B02). Add other tests if the end use needs them.

Who should pay for testing?

It is negotiable and should be agreed in the order. Many buyers require the supplier to provide a report for the production lot and reserve the right to run independent tests.

Conclusion

Tests turn a fabric description into a contract. Pick the properties that matter for your end use, cite the standard and conditions, set pass limits, and keep a retained sample. That is what makes a repeat order consistent.
