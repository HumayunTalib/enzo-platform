PV & Blended Fabrics

Polyester Viscose (PV) Fabric: Blend Ratios, Properties and Uses

By ENZO Textiles · Published 4 November 2026 · 7 min read

Polyester viscose fabric — usually shortened to PV, poly-viscose or, in some markets, TR — is woven from yarn in which polyester staple fibre and viscose staple fibre are blended together before spinning. The polyester brings strength, shape retention and easy care; the viscose brings softness, drape and moisture absorbency. Which of those wins depends almost entirely on the blend ratio and the construction.

What is PV fabric?

PV is a blend of a synthetic fibre (polyester, made from petroleum-based polymer) and a regenerated cellulosic fibre (viscose, made from dissolved wood pulp and re-formed into fibre). Because viscose is cellulose, it is often sold as rayon in some markets; the two names describe the same fibre family. In trade usage, the abbreviation TR is also used for polyester–rayon fabric, as distinct from TC, which is polyester–cotton (Iyunai Textile).

In an intimate blend the two staple fibres are mixed before or during spinning, so every yarn carries both. This differs from a union fabric, where one yarn system is polyester and the other is viscose. When a buyer asks for a "65/35 PV" they normally mean an intimate blend yarn in both warp and weft, but you should confirm this in writing.

Common PV blend ratios

A technical reference on blended fabrics gives the usual range for polyester–viscose as roughly 65/35 down through 55/45 to 48/52, and notes that 48/52 and 65/35 are the ratios most commonly used for school uniforms and suiting (Textile School). Marketplace listings show the same pattern, with 65/35 and 70/30 suiting cloth widely offered (GlobalLinker; IndiaMART).

How PV fabric behaves

What polyester contributes

Polyester has a very low moisture regain — about 0.4% under the commercial regain table in ASTM D1909 — so it absorbs almost no water into the fibre. That helps the fabric dry quickly and hold its shape, but it also means pure polyester feels less comfortable against skin in heat. Training material from UNIDO also notes that polyester has poor pilling behaviour compared with cotton (UNIDO), which matters for surfaces that are rubbed often, such as trousers and uniforms.

What viscose contributes

Viscose absorbs far more moisture than polyester — ASTM D1909 lists rayon at 11.0%, and other published fibre data put viscose at about 13% — which gives the blend a cooler, softer hand than pure polyester. The trade-off is that viscose loses strength when wet (Testex) and is described as having poor dimensional stability (Testex). In practice that means PV fabric should be tested for shrinkage after washing rather than assumed stable; see our guide to fabric quality tests and ISO standards.

Why construction matters as much as ratio

Published research on plain-woven PV fabrics treats moisture transmission as a function of blend proportion, yarn count and twist together (Das, Studies on moisture transmission properties of PV-blended fabrics), which reflects that the ratio is only one of several variables. Two PV fabrics with the same 65/35 label can therefore feel very different. This is why a serious specification also states yarn count, ends and picks per inch, weave and weight.

What a PV specification looks like

A published trade specification sheet for PV 65/35 shows how constructions are normally written: warp count and blend, weft count and blend, EPI × PPI and weave — for example, a 2/40 PV warp with a 20s PV weft at 84 × 64 in a 2/1 twill, alongside plain-weave options at 72 × 56 (Fibre2Fashion). Listings in a single marketplace category for PV cloth show a wide spread of weights, from about 80 GSM to over 300 GSM, which is a reminder that "PV" alone says nothing about weight (IndiaMART). To estimate the weight of a construction, see how to calculate fabric GSM from EPI, PPI and yarn count.

How PV fabric is dyed

Because the two fibres are chemically different, PV cannot be dyed with a single dye class. Polyester is dyed with disperse dyes at high temperature; viscose is dyed with dyes suited to cellulose, typically reactive dyes. Published work on PV describes dyeing the polyester portion first and then the viscose portion in a separate step (NMIMS study on a 48/52 PV fabric), and one-bath, two-stage methods have been studied to shorten the process (Scientific.net). Patent literature on polyester/natural-fibre blends makes the same point: such blends typically need both disperse and reactive dyes (US 12325955).

For a buyer the consequence is practical: shade matching on PV takes two dye systems to line up, so shade tolerance, lab dips and colour-fastness requirements should be agreed before bulk production — not after.

Typical uses

  • Suiting and trousers — the main use for 65/35 and 55/45 constructions with a firm hand.
  • Uniforms — school, office and security uniforms, where easy care and shape retention matter (see fabric for uniforms).
  • Formal and occasion wear — where drape and a clean surface are priorities (see fabric for formal wear).

What to specify when ordering PV in bulk

  1. Blend ratio and tolerance — for example 65/35 ± an agreed percentage, confirmed by lab fibre analysis if it matters commercially.
  2. Yarn count and type in warp and weft — including whether yarns are single or plied.
  3. Construction — EPI × PPI and weave (plain, twill and so on).
  4. Weight and width — GSM with a tolerance, and cuttable width; see GSM and width: how they change cost per metre.
  5. Performance tests — dimensional change after washing, pilling, colour fastness and tensile strength, with the standard named.
  6. Shade approval process — lab dip, then strike-off or bulk approval.

Frequently asked questions

Is PV fabric the same as polyester cotton?

No. PV blends polyester with viscose (regenerated cellulose from wood pulp); polyester cotton blends polyester with cotton. They behave differently — see polyester viscose vs polyester cotton.

What is the most common PV blend ratio?

Sources describing blended suiting and uniform cloth give 65/35 and 48/52 as the commonly used polyester–viscose ratios, with 55/45 also in the usual range (Textile School).

Is PV fabric breathable?

More than pure polyester, because viscose absorbs moisture, but a blend's moisture regain still sits well below that of pure viscose or cotton. Fabric construction affects the result as much as the ratio.

Does PV fabric shrink?

The viscose component can make PV dimensionally less stable than polyester alone, so shrinkage should be measured with a standard test method and a tolerance agreed in the order.

Is PV the same as TR fabric?

In trade usage, TR commonly refers to polyester–rayon, and rayon and viscose are the same fibre family, so PV and TR often describe the same type of cloth. Always confirm the composition in writing.

What GSM is PV fabric?

There is no single GSM. Marketplace listings for PV range from about 80 to over 300 GSM depending on yarn count and construction.

Conclusion

PV is a flexible fabric family, not one product. Treat the ratio as the starting point of the specification, then pin down yarn count, construction, weight, shade and test requirements. That is what separates a repeatable bulk order from a surprise.
