PV & Blended Fabrics

What Is Poly Linen Fabric? Polyester-Linen Blends vs Linen-Look Polyester

By ENZO Textiles · Published 4 November 2026 · 4 min read

“Poly linen” is not a single, standardised fabric. In the trade it is used for two different things: a polyester–linen blend that contains real flax fibre, and a linen-look fabric that is woven from polyester (often slub-effect yarn) to imitate linen’s texture. They look similar on a swatch and behave differently in wear, which is why the composition on the specification matters.

The two meanings of poly linen

1. Polyester–linen blend

A blended linen and polyester fabric combines linen’s textured look with polyester’s strength and practicality. Suppliers describe it as less prone to wrinkling than pure linen, more resistant to machine washing and quicker to dry without losing shape (Cimmino). The linen content is what gives the blend its natural texture and moisture behaviour.

2. Linen-look polyester

Some suppliers sell “linen-look” cloth made from 100% polyester, using yarns with irregular slub effects to reproduce the surface of linen (CIFF guide to linen-look vs linen). This product has no flax in it. It can be a legitimate, lower-cost, easy-care fabric — but it should be specified and priced as polyester, not as a linen blend.

How the fibres differ

The fibre-level numbers explain why the two kinds of poly linen feel different against the skin. Commercial moisture regain values in ASTM D1909 list polyester at 0.4% and raw flax at 12.0%. Using the weighted-average method for blends (NPTEL):

FabricEstimated fibre-level regain
100% polyester linen-look0.4%
Illustrative 50/50 polyester–linen blend0.5×0.4 + 0.5×12.0 ≈ 6.2%
Pure linen (flax)≈12%

The 50/50 ratio is an illustration, not a statement about any particular product. Real comfort also depends on yarn, construction and weight.

The practical reading: a true polyester–linen blend sits between the two extremes and keeps some of linen’s moisture handling, whereas a 100% polyester linen-look fabric behaves like polyester. Polyester’s low regain is also associated with a dry, slippery feel and a tendency to build static (Testex). See moisture regain explained for how these numbers are used.

What to ask before ordering poly linen in bulk

  1. Exact fibre composition — is there flax in it, and what percentage? If composition matters commercially, request a lab fibre analysis rather than relying on a label.
  2. Yarn type — slub, plain, spun or filament, and the yarn count in warp and weft.
  3. Construction — EPI × PPI, weave and weight. Open constructions behave very differently from dense ones; for example, an 80 × 72 construction is lighter in density than a 112 × 84 construction.
  4. Weight and width — GSM with tolerance, and cuttable width. To estimate weight from a construction, see how to calculate fabric GSM.
  5. Shrinkage and colour-fastness results — under named standards, as covered in fabric quality tests for bulk buyers.
  6. Dyeing method and shade tolerance — blends containing polyester need disperse dyes for the polyester part and a second dye class for the natural-fibre part.

Typical uses

Lighter, textured poly-linen cloth is commonly chosen for warm-season garments where a linen look is wanted with easier care. For season-specific guidance see summer vs winter fabric for men; for a general comparison method see how to compare two fabrics side by side.

Frequently asked questions

Does poly linen contain real linen?

Sometimes. A polyester–linen blend does; a linen-look polyester fabric does not. The composition declared on the specification is the only reliable answer.

Is poly linen breathable?

It depends on the product. Polyester has a very low moisture regain, so 100% polyester linen-look cloth behaves like polyester, while a blend containing linen retains some of linen’s moisture handling. Construction and weight also matter.

Does poly linen wrinkle?

Supplier guides describe polyester–linen blends as less wrinkle-prone than pure linen, and polyester-based linen-look cloth as typically wrinkle-resistant. Test crease behaviour on the actual fabric if it matters.

Is poly linen cheaper than pure linen?

Polyester is generally the lower-cost component, but price depends on composition, yarn, construction and finishing. Compare on a like-for-like weight and width basis.

How can I check what is in a poly linen fabric?

Ask for a lab fibre-composition analysis. A burn test or hand feel is not a substitute when the claim has commercial consequences.

Conclusion

Treat “poly linen” as a category, not a composition. Before ordering, pin down whether the cloth contains linen, what the ratio is, and how the construction and weight match your end use. That one question separates a blend from an imitation.
