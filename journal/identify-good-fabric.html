How to Identify Good Fabric: A Practical Checklist

Cluster: Buying Guides · Primary keyword: how to identify good fabric quality

"Good fabric" isn't one property — it's several, and which ones matter most depends entirely on what the fabric is for. This is a practical checklist for judging a swatch or a roll with nothing more than your hands, your eyes, and a few direct questions.

1. Hand feel

Rub the fabric between your fingers. Does it feel dry and crisp, or smooth and fluid? Neither is "better" — but it should match what you're buying it for. A hand that feels rough or scratchy on skin-adjacent garments is a real problem regardless of how good the construction numbers look on paper.

2. Construction density

Check the reed and pick — see our full explainer on what reed and pick mean. A pick glass makes this a 30-second check: count ends and picks per inch and compare against what was quoted. A construction that doesn't match its own specification sheet is the single clearest quality red flag there is.

3. Crease recovery

Crush a section of fabric in your fist for a few seconds, then release. Fabric that returns close to flat within a minute has good elastic recovery. Fabric that stays creased has poor recovery — a real problem for anything that gets sat in, packed, or worn for a full day.

4. Colorfastness

Rub a white cloth firmly against the fabric's surface (a basic crocking test). Colour transfer onto the white cloth means the dye isn't well fixed, and it will bleed onto skin, other garments in the wash, or upholstery. This matters more on darker and more saturated shades.

5. Shade consistency across the piece

Check the shade at both selvedges and the centre of the fabric, and compare the beginning of the roll to the end. Genuine dye-lot consistency should hold across the whole piece — visible drift is a sign of an inconsistent dyeing process.

6. Opacity and cover

Hold the fabric up to a light source. Gaps or thin patches in the weave show up immediately this way, even on fabric that looks fine under normal room light.

7. Weight matches the claim

If a GSM figure was quoted, weigh a measured piece and check it — see what GSM actually measures for the calculation. This is the fastest way to catch a fabric that's been substituted for a lighter, cheaper version of the same name.

The single most useful habit: ask for the construction, GSM, and composition every time — not just the fabric's trade name. A name tells you a category; the numbers tell you what you're actually holding.

Quick reference checklist

  • Hand feel matches the intended use
  • Reed/pick matches the quoted construction
  • Fabric recovers from a crush test within a minute
  • No visible colour transfer on a crocking check
  • Shade consistent across the full piece
  • No visible gaps when held to light
  • Weighed GSM matches the quoted figure

Frequently asked questions

Can I judge fabric quality without any tools?

Mostly, yes — hand feel, crush recovery, colour transfer, and holding fabric to light all require nothing but your hands and eyes. A pick glass and a small scale help but aren't strictly required for a first pass.

Is a higher price always a sign of better quality?

No — see our piece on quality vs price. Price should track construction, yarn, and finish; check those directly rather than trusting price alone.
