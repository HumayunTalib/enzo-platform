Reed and Pick Explained: How Fabric Density Is Measured

Cluster: Terminology · Primary keyword: what is reed and pick

Ask a mill what a fabric's "construction" is and the answer comes back as two numbers, a multiplication sign, and a unit — something like 108 × 76. That pair is reed and pick, and it's the single most useful shorthand for how dense a woven fabric actually is.

Reed: the warp-direction count

Reed refers to the number of warp yarns (the yarns running the length of the fabric, set up on the loom before weaving starts) packed into a fixed width — traditionally counted per unit of the loom's reed, the comb-like device that spaces the warp yarns and beats each weft pick into place. In practice, "reed count" is used interchangeably with ends per inch in most commercial conversations, though the two aren't always numerically identical depending on how many yarns are drawn through each dent of the reed.

Pick: the weft-direction count

Pick is the equivalent count in the other direction — the number of weft yarns (the yarns the shuttle or rapier inserts across the fabric, over and under the warp) per inch. Every pass of weft across the loom is one pick; picks per inch is a direct measurement of how many of those passes are packed into each inch of finished fabric.

The construction number, read correctly: "108 × 76" means 108 warp ends per inch and 76 weft picks per inch. Higher numbers in both directions generally mean a denser, heavier, more durable cloth — but the two numbers don't have to move together, and a fabric can be dense in one direction and open in the other.

Why this matters more than the fabric's name

Two suppliers can both call a fabric "boski" or "suiting twill" while quoting completely different constructions. The name describes a category; reed and pick describe the actual object. When comparing quotes, ask for both numbers before you ask about price — see our guide on warp vs weft for how the two yarn systems interact to produce this count.

How ends and picks are actually counted

Both are checked with a pick glass (a small magnifier with a marked window, typically one inch or half an inch) placed on the fabric surface. Count the individual yarns crossing the window in each direction and scale to a full inch. This is a hand-tool check anyone can do on a sample — it doesn't require lab equipment, which is why it's the first thing an experienced buyer does when a swatch arrives.

What reed and pick affect downstream

  • Weight (GSM). Higher ends and picks per inch, all else equal, pack more yarn into the same area — see our note on what GSM actually measures.
  • Cost. More yarn per metre means more material and more loom time — reed and pick are direct inputs into fabric costing.
  • Hand and drape. A denser construction generally feels firmer and holds its shape more; a more open one moves more freely.
  • Durability. Denser constructions typically resist abrasion and pilling better.

Frequently asked questions

Is reed the same as ends per inch?

They're used interchangeably in most commercial conversations, though technically reed count refers to the loom's reed specification and can differ slightly from the finished ends-per-inch count depending on draw-in.

Do higher reed and pick numbers always mean better quality?

They mean denser, not automatically better — a lighter, more open construction can be exactly right for a summer fabric. Match the construction to the intended use rather than assuming higher is superior.
